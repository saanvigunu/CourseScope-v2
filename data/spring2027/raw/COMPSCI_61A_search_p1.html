Robotics has been late to the deep learning revolution compared to computer vision and natural language processing, mainly because ”big data” is not so readily available. However significant advances have been made in the last few years, and the purpose of this class is to present a coherent framework for studying these advances. My goal is to build machines that can emulate the remarkable capabilities of humans and other animals at motor control, defined as connecting perception to action in the physical world. Hence there will be a distinct preference for legs over wheels, multi-finger hands over parallel jaw grippers, rich visual and tactile perception over minimal sensing, humanoid robots in the home over specialist robots on the factory floor. The course itself will have three main parts: (1) biological motor control basics for inspiration (2) main paradigms for robot motor skill acquisition (3) case studies of locomotion, navigation and manipulation. This is a graduate level class, and prerequisite knowledge at the level of Deep Learning (Bishop & Bishop), and Reinforcement Learning (Sutton & Barto) will be assumed. Grounded perception and action are key capabilities of future AI systems, enabling machines to understand, reason about, predict, and interact with the physical world. This course explores the interplay of vision, language, world models, and action in contemporary large-scale multimodal and agentic AI systems. Topics include foundational vision-language models, generative models for image and video understanding and prediction, vision-language-action models, and world action models that connect perception with physical reasoning and control. The course will examine emerging approaches to agentic intelligence, including planning and reasoning with large models, code generation, reusable skills, and the integration of predictive world models with embodied decision-making. Additional topics may include reinforcement learning, multimodal alignment, scaling laws, continual and long-context learning, and learning from human and robot interaction data. The course will also address interpretability, robustness, and ethical considerations in the development of autonomous AI systems. The overarching goal is to equip students with the conceptual and technical foundations to contribute to research on multimodal, embodied, and agentic AI systems capable of reasoning about and acting in the world.

Directed Group Studies for Advanced Undergraduates

GamesCrafters: Computational Game Theory Research and Development
Dan Garcia
Jan 19, 2027 - May 07, 2027
Mo, We, Fr
11:00 am - 11:59 am
The Gateway Building B1016
Class #:28597
Units:1to4

Instruction Mode: In-Person Instruction

No Open Seats
The GamesCrafters group explores the fertile area of combinatorial and computational puzzle and game theory. At the core of the project is GAMESMAN, a system developed for solving, playing and analyzing two-person, abstract strategy games (e.g., Tic-Tac-Toe, or Chess) and one-person puzzles (e.g., Sokoban, or Klotski). Given the description of a game or puzzle as input, our system generates a text-based and graphical application that will solve it (in the strong sense), and then play it perfectly. Programmers can easily prototype a new game or puzzle with multiple rule variants, learn the strategy via color-coded moves, and perform extended analysis. Since its inception, more than one hundred games and puzzles have been integrated into the system by over eight hundred undergraduates, with still more in development. While most students implemented new games and puzzles, others modified the core architecture, wrote game-specific optimal hash functions, and added databases to increase the program's speed and efficiency. Designing intuitive and aesthetic graphical user interfaces has also been popular project. Our future research direction is principally "hunting big game/puzzle" — implementing, solving, and analyzing large games/puzzles whose perfect strategy is yet unknown.
